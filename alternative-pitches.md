Same product: a camera reads the level in a tank, a person in frame is the visit, and a custom block checks the ticket against the before and the after. The $2,000–$3,100 over five years against WellAware is a cheaper monitor. It is a weak reason to buy. None of the pitches below invent a miss rate, a failure rate, or a well count. Each one changes the unit of money to something already sourced, and states the hurdle that would have to be true.

The batch truck is still out. Murphy's inhibitor goes down the annulus. There is no tank. Those trips are not for sale.

## Pitch A — The operator's loop. Recommended.

Patricia is one loop, not the basin. A loop here is a set of wells whose production goes to the same tank battery and whose chemical is managed together. This one is 14 wells near Patricia, Texas, in Dawson County, on the northern shelf of the Permian. It is not the Delaware. The wells are conventional verticals, about 22 barrels of oil and 59 barrels of water a day, on a pressurized chemical tank, one blend per well. The paper is from a chemical company, the trial ran from October 2014 into March 2015, and the average cost was $16.89 a well a day ([SWPSC](https://www.swpshortcourse.org/paper/2015025-improving-paraffin-treating-modernizing-chemical)).

That is the only loop in the file with a well count, a daily chemical cost, and a tank that drains fast enough to see. It is an example of the shape. It is not a census of Delaware or Permian operators. The same paper's vendor had put the tank system on over 1,500 wells across the Permian. That count mixes programs. It is not 1,500 copies of this $16.89 rate, and it is not a license to multiply by the basin.

**Customer.** The operator who already pays for chemical into tanks on a loop like this. Not a batch route. Not a 0.020 gallon-a-day stripper.

**The check.** 14 × $16.89 × 365 = **$86,300 a year** for this one loop.

**The cameras.** One per well, because each well had its own tank. Fourteen boxes are about $15,000–$31,000 over five years, about $3,100–$6,200 a year.

**The hurdle.** $3,100 / $86,300 = 3.6%. $6,200 / $86,300 = 7.2%. The pictures pay for themselves if they move this loop's chemical bill by about **4–7% a year**. A half-load on a tank draining at 0.8 gallons a day is outside the daylight error, so a short fill of that size would count. I do not know how often one happens. A 90-day pilot on the loop is what counts them.

**Any other operator, same formula.** Their wells on the loop, times their dollars per well per day, times 365. Cameras at one per tank. The hurdle is camera cost divided by that chemical bill. A Delaware pad with several wells and a higher daily rate makes the hurdle smaller. A stripper loop makes it worse, and the level error hides the miss, so that loop stays out. I do not have a Delaware loop's chemical bill, so I do not invent one.

**What this is not.** It is not 918,000 wells. It is not Murphy's 700 batch wells. It is not "every operator in the Delaware." One company can have many loops. You sell the next loop with that operator's own ticket, after the first pilot.

**Say this.** I'm not sizing the Permian. I'm sizing one loop, because that is the check an operator signs. Near Patricia, fourteen wells on tanks, about $86,000 a year in chemical, draining fast enough that a short fill shows up. Fourteen cameras are about $3,000–$6,000 a year, 4–7% of that bill. If the pictures move the renewal by that much, they pay for themselves. I don't know the miss rate. A Delaware operator gives me their loop and I run the same division. I don't use this fourteen as their fourteen.

## Pitch B — The chemical company keeps the loop.

**Customer.** The service company that sells the chemical on that same loop. The $86,300 is their revenue, not their cost.

**What changed.** One retained year of the loop is $86,300 of revenue against about $3,100–$6,200 a year of cameras. That is on the order of fourteen years of cameras for one year of the loop. I do not know their margin, and I do not know if the pictures are why they keep the bid.

**Why it can fail in the room.** A camera they own puts their picture back on their invoice. That is the pad as it already works. Use this only if the buyer is the company trying to prove a delivery the operator disputes. Otherwise stay with Pitch A.

**Say this.** The same fourteen wells are $86,000 a year of their revenue. The cameras are about 3.6–7% of that each year. If the pictures are the reason they keep the loop for one more year, the boxes are paid for many times. I don't know that they are the reason.

## Pitch C — One camera on the battery tank.

**Customer.** The operator, at the tank battery, where one vessel was taking 40 gallons a day.

**What changed.** One box, not fourteen. The sourced stream was about $133,000 over five months. Run for a year at the trial price, that is about $321,000. The box is about $2,200 over five years, which is under **1% of one year** of that stream. A half-load on that vessel is far outside a ±10–17 gallon error.

**The limit.** That field shut the 40 gallons a day off, because the wellhead blend made it unnecessary. The camera did not do that. The pitch is only for a battery that still injects tens of gallons a day. I do not know how many still do.

**Say this.** The big chemical dollar in that paper was not the wellhead jug. It was 40 gallons a day at the battery, about $133,000 in one season. One camera on that vessel is pointed at a six-figure stream. I would sell that tank, not a stripper.

## Pitch D — One half-load pays for one box.

**Customer.** Same operator, one fast tank, no fleet required.

**What changed.** The demo's own event is the unit. Half of a 335-gallon refill is about 125 gallons, about **$2,760** at $22 a gallon. The expensive box is about $2,200 over five years. One short fill of that size pays for the box. A 5–10 gallon pad does not. It sits inside the error. A 10-gallon truck batch does not either.

**Say this.** I am not asking you to believe a rate. I am asking you to look at one ticket. Half of a 335-gallon fill is about $2,800. That is the box. If that ticket never happens, the camera was a cheaper monitor and nothing more.

## What I would actually say

Pitch A, with Pitch D as the demo sentence and Pitch C as the answer if they say the wellhead jug is too small. Pitch B only if they ask who else pays.

Do not say the 918,000 wells are the market. Do not put Murphy's $320–$720 on this camera. Do not put one pad in twenty into the payback. The hurdle on the loop is the new term. The miss rate is still missing, and the pitch says so.
